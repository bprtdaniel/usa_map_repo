Loss of Native American Land

Why the Story of U.S. Territorial Evolution is too often told as an American Adventure

As the United States approaches 250 years since its founding, the familiar story begins with thirteen colonies declaring independence in 1776. Eight years and a Revolutionary War later, Britain recognized U.S. independence in the Treaty of Paris.

But the land Britain recognized as part of the United States was not empty, and Native nations had not agreed to its transfer. What is often framed as a handover of tribal territory was, in reality, the start of a long and brutal conflict against Native people to gain control of their land.

With the signing of the treaty, a long period of westward U.S. expansion began. Often referred to as 'Manifest Destiny', this expansion followed the widespread belief that American settlers were supposedly divinely destined to expand their territory across the continent.

Be it history class, paintings, or animations, we are often presented with expansion toward the West from the settler's view. These narratives often show how the U.S. formed more and more states, formalizing the Union.

What is less often visualized comprehensively is how this expansion came about what it meant from the perspective of Native nations: War, land loss, military violence, forced treaties, displacement, and removal.

This scrollytelling map presents a non-exhaustive visual account of several major steps in Native land loss, aiming to provide an alternative to the U.S. narrative.

Recorded Indigenous land cessions

The green area shows recorded Indigenous land cessions in the dataset that was used.

It is not a complete map of all Native presence and land before 1783, but serves as a representation.

01

Native nations east of the Mississippi

Many native tribes inhabited the area east of the Mississippi, which was under British control up until 1783, the purple area to the right of the green.

Tribes include the Cherokees, Creeks, Choctaws, Chickasaws, and Seminoles in the southern part, and moving further to the north, Shawnees, Delawares, Mingoes, Ottawas, Wyandots, Miamis, Potawatomis, Kickapoos, Chippewas, and others.

Historians estimate that 200,000 - 250,000 native Americans inhabited the area east of the Mississippi.

02

Treaty of Paris

The United States gains independence and claims territory east of the Mississippi. From 1783 to 1803, these lands formalize into the first 17 states of the Union.

But the Treaty of Paris was an agreement between Britain and the United States, not a legitimate transfer of Native land. Native nations living there were not consulted.

The treaty gave the U.S. a paper claim, not uncontested possession. Turning that claim into territorial control required either forced treaties with the native tribes or military force.

03

Tecumseh’s War and the Treaty of Fort Wayne

An example of Indian resistance is the Treaty of Fort Wayne in 1809 and its effects on the present-day midwest area of Ohio and Indiana.

03B

Fort Wayne Treaty

Negotiated by William Henry Harrison, the treaty transferred roughly 3 million acres of native land of several tribes to the United States.

03C

Conflict Area of Tecumseh’s War

Shawnee tribe leader Tecumseh rejected the treaty, establishing a loose confederacy of tribal nations that fought in resistance against Harrison's troops. At the Battle of Tippecanoe, Tecumseh's resistance was defeated by US military forces, losing control over the territory.

04

1803 Louisiana Purchase

The U.S. buys roughly 2 million square kilometers from France, the largest land acquisition of its expansion.

The purchase created a massive U.S. imperial claim west of the Mississippi. But it did not create actual, uncontested control over the land.

These lands were already inhabited by Native nations and, as before, U.S. claims would become control only through treaties and military force.

04B

Lewis and Clark Expedition

Another "US-centric" aspect of the Louisiana Purchase are the Lewis and Clark expeditions, often framed as heroic explorers who “opened” the West.

But the expedition served as an intelligence gathering mission, intended to prepare to gain control over native land via treaties and military force.

05

War of 1812 as a War on Native Americans

The War of 1812 is mostly remembered as the American-British war, set in the wider context of the Napoleonic wars in Europe. It was, however, also a series of wars against Native nations resisting U.S. expansion.

U.S. forces fought Tecumseh’s confederacy in the midwest, Sauk, Meskwaki, Kickapoo, Red Stick Creeks, Seminoles, and Native allies of Britain.

After defeating the Red Stick Creeks, the U.S. imposed the Treaty of Fort Jackson in 1814. The Creek Nation was forced to cede 23 million acres in the Southeast.

06

Seminole Wars and Treaty Wave

After the War of 1812 and the defeat of several Native resistance movements, the United States used its strengthened military and diplomatic position to secure a new wave of land cessions. Between 1816 and 1823, the U.S. signed land-cession treaties with eighteen different tribes.

The 1818 treaty with the Delaware tribe became the first to completely remove a tribe to reservation lands west of the Mississippi.

In present-day Florida, renewed war against the Seminoles helped pressure Spain to cede the peninsula to the United States in 1819.

07

Indian Removal Act

The Indian Removal Act of 1830 is often remembered as the beginning of Native removal, largely because it led to the Trail of Tears. But removal did not begin in 1830.

By then, the United States had already used war, treaty pressure, settler encroachment, and state-level land grabs to force Native land cessions across the East and Southeast.

What changed in 1830 was that removal became an explicit federal program, which is mostly remembered today. The law authorized the president to negotiate land exchanges with Native nations east of the Mississippi for lands farther west.

08

Trail of Tears

Native Resistance to the Removal Act was widespread, but gradually broken and removal was enforced with military power.

More than 16,000 Cherokee Indian People were evicted from their homelands in Tennessee, Alabama and Georgia, first sent to "round-up camps" and then towards territory west of the Mississippi.

This created what is widely-known as the "Trail of Tears", with more than 1000 Cherokees dying on their forced expulsion westwards. Including additional tribes that were expulsed, estimates range to 4000 deaths.

The tribes were forced towards "Indian Territory", as the removal treaties contained the provision that the new land was protected for Native Americans.

10

Mexican Cession and Broken Promises of Protected Land

The Mexican Cession after the U.S.- Mexican War expanded U.S. claims into the Southwest and Pacific West. This brought present-day California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming under U.S. control.

This expansion weakened the idea that Native nations could be permanently separated from U.S. settlement in a western Indian Territory. The West itself was becoming the next target of U.S. expansion.

The promise of protected Native land was repeatedly broken as settlers, military roads, trade routes, mining, and later railroads pushed farther into Native lands.

11

Reservation policy era

By the mid-nineteenth century, U.S. policy shifted away from one broad Indian Territory toward a system of smaller, bounded reservations.

The 1851 Indian Appropriations Act formalized this reservation system. Reservations were presented as protected Native lands, but they also made Native communities easier for the federal government to supervise and control.

Treaties such as the 1851 Fort Laramie Treaty show the logic of this shift: Native reservations were promised in treaties, yet they were almost immediately broken, with white settlers moving into the treaty's established territories.

In practice, reservations restricted movement, disrupted hunting and food systems, and were still often forcefully inhabited by white settlers.

12

Dawes Act / allotment era

After Native nations were confined to reservations, U.S. policy shifted again: instead of only reducing reservations from the outside, the government began breaking them apart from within.

The Dawes Act of 1887 divided collectively held tribal reservation land into individual allotments. Native people were pushed to register on federal rolls, accept private parcels, and adopt Euro-American farming as part of an assimilation policy.

Land that remained after allotment was labeled “surplus” and opened to non-Native settlers. In practice, a policy presented as protecting Native property became another major mechanism of land loss.

By breaking communal landholding and selling so-called surplus land, allotment stripped Native nations of more than 90 million acres of tribal land.

13

Present-day reservations

Today, the Bureau of Indian Affairs counts about 326 Indian land areas administered as federal Indian reservations, pueblos, rancherias, missions, villages, and communities.

These lands are not a complete map of Native presence, sovereignty, or historical homelands. Some are remnants of original land bases; others were created after forced relocation.

This map ends by challenging the usual U.S.-centered story of westward expansion as exploration or Manifest Destiny. Seen from Native nations, it was also a history of war, forced treaties, broken promises, deadly removal, and the continuous reduction of Native land.

This map distinguishes recorded historical land cessions from present-day reservations. These layers should not be read as a single continuous boundary of Indigenous territory.
